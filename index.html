Research interests

We study the evolutionary genomics of Salicaceae and other woody plants. Poplars, willows, and their relatives are ecologically and economically important trees with repeated origins of dioecy, frequent hybridization, and polyploidy, making them an excellent system for linking genome evolution to sex, adaptation, and diversification.

Sex determination and sex chromosome evolution

Dioecy has evolved repeatedly in angiosperms. We compare sex chromosomes across Populus, Salix, Hippophae, and related lineages to study the origin of sex-determining regions, recombination suppression, heteromorphism, degeneration, dosage compensation, and turnover, as well as the genetic basis of sexual dimorphism. Recent work identified a two-gene sex-determination system in Salicaceae and recurrent turnovers of XY and ZW systems in willows and sea buckthorn.

Genome evolution, hybridization and polyploidy

We use phylogenomics, population genomics, and pangenomes to reconstruct relationships across Salicaceae and ask how hybridization, introgression, incomplete lineage sorting, whole-genome duplication, and allopolyploidy contribute to speciation and adaptive radiation. We are particularly interested in how subgenomes evolve after polyploidization and how ancient and recent gene flow shape genomic landscapes of divergence.

Environmental adaptation and functional genomics

We study how woody plants respond to drought, salinity, nutrient limitation, and biotic stress. By combining natural population variation, transcriptomics, chromatin accessibility, and genetic manipulation, we identify regulatory modules — including transcription factors, E3 ligases, and chromatin regulators — that control stress tolerance and growth trade-offs in poplar.

Single-cell and spatial omics

We develop and apply single-cell, single-nucleus, and spatial transcriptomic approaches to resolve cell types and developmental trajectories in woody tissues, including stems, leaves, and shoot apices. This work provides a foundation for understanding wood formation, leaf development, and cell-type-specific responses to the environment.

Genome resources and conservation

We assemble and annotate high-quality genomes for Salicaceae and other ecologically and economically important plants, and use population genomics to inform conservation of endangered trees. We also build community resources, such as the Salicaceae Single-Cell Atlas.
