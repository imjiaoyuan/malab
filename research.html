Research directions

Salicaceae includes poplars, willows, and their relatives — ecologically and economically important trees that occupy habitats from temperate forests to tropical riversides. Dioecy, long generation times, frequent hybridization, and polyploidy make this family an excellent system for studying sex evolution, environmental adaptation, and speciation.

Starting from natural variation, we combine comparative genomics, population genetics, and functional genomics to connect genotype, phenotype, and environment.

Sex determination and sex chromosome evolution

Dioecy has evolved repeatedly in angiosperms. We compare sex chromosomes across Populus, Salix, and related lineages to study the origin of sex-determining regions, recombination suppression, degeneration, and turnover, as well as the genetic basis of sexual dimorphism.

Genomic basis of environmental adaptation

We study drought, salinity, and cold tolerance in natural populations and experimental systems, linking genetic variation and gene regulation to adaptation, and examining the roles of gene flow and natural selection.

Speciation and diversification

We use phylogenomics and population genomics to reconstruct relationships across Salicaceae and ask how hybridization, polyploidy, incomplete lineage sorting, and ancient gene flow contribute to speciation and radiation.

Genome structure and chromatin regulation

We examine chromatin accessibility, 3D genome organization, histone modifications, and DNA methylation to understand how regulatory elements shape development, stress responses, and species divergence.

Methods

Our work combines chromosome-level genome assembly and annotation, comparative and population genomics, epigenomics (Hi-C, ATAC-seq, WGBS, ChIP-seq), single-cell and spatial omics, molecular experiments and transformation, and data integration and visualization.
